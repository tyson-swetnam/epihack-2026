EpiHack AZ 2026

Q1 — Cooling-center awareness and interactive tools

How do you inform the public about the location of cooling centers? Are there interactive tools for the public to use?

Starting context

Arizona already has a multi-layered cooling-center communication infrastructure, with the Maricopa Association of Governments (MAG) Heat Relief Network and the statewide ADHS Heat Preparedness Network as the two most visible interactive layers.

Statewide interactive tools

Tool What it shows Operator
ADHS Heat Preparedness Network map Statewide ArcGIS Experience map of Heat Preparedness Network locations Arizona Department of Health Services
MAG Heat Relief Network 200+ cooling, hydration, and respite locations in the Phoenix metro; filters by services, hours, pet-friendly Maricopa Association of Governments
Pima County Cooling Centers map Tucson / Pima County cooling centers and hydration stations Pima County Health Department
UA Center for Rural Health — AZ Cooling Centers Map Statewide, with rural emphasis University of Arizona
211 Arizona — Heat Relief Phone-first (call 211 or 1-877-211-8661); English / Spanish operators 9 a.m. – 7 p.m. daily; transportation arrangement and emergency A/C repair referrals Solari Crisis & Human Services

Operating window

Cooling-center networks operate May 1 through September 30 each year. 211 Arizona is available year-round for heat resources.

Communication channels used today

  • Interactive ArcGIS web maps (the two key ones above).
  • 211 phone line and SMS in English and Spanish.
  • City library systems acting as cooling centers; some open until 10 p.m. (e.g. Harmon, Cholla, Yucca in Phoenix).
  • Clear Channel Outdoor + Maricopa County digital-billboard partnership displays heat-relief resources during the season.
  • Local news refresh cycles each May; ABC15 / 12News / azcentral.
  • The Governor’s annually-launched Heat Relief Plan announcement serves as a season kickoff.

Gaps the team could target

  • Real-time space and supply availability is not shown on the public maps — see Q2.
  • Unsheltered residents, who bear the highest mortality burden, often lack a phone, charge, or data plan to use the web maps; the channels that reach them are CHWs, outreach vans, and word of mouth.
  • Indigenous-language materials are limited; tribal cooling resources are inconsistently represented on regional maps.
  • Transportation gaps to cooling centers are a leading reason a map doesn’t translate into use.
  • Cross-county discovery is fragmented: a person near a county line may miss the nearer center because each county runs its own map.
