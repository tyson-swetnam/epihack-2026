Q4 — Who is most vulnerable to heat in Arizona?

Who are most vulnerable to heat in AZ?

What the data shows

Drawing from MCDPH heat-associated death reports (2006–present), ADHS state heat-mortality reports (2013–2024), and academic syntheses:

Headline numbers

  • More than 4,320 people have died from heat exposure in Arizona between 2013 and 2024.
  • ~4,298 emergency-room visits per year for heat-related illness in AZ.
  • 990 heat-related deaths in 2023 statewide.
  • Maricopa County alone has run a dedicated heat-mortality surveillance system since 2006.

Highest-burden groups

Group Evidence
People experiencing unsheltered homelessness Largest single factor in the post-2010 rise in Maricopa heat deaths. The unsheltered share of heat-associated deaths reached 36% in 2016 in Maricopa County and has remained elevated.
Older adults (65+) Disproportionate share of indoor heat deaths, often linked to AC failure or unaffordability.
American Indian / Alaska Native and African American Maricopa residents Highest heat-associated death rates per 100,000 in MCDPH data.
Males 81% of all heat-associated deaths in Maricopa County.
Outdoor workers Landscape, construction, agriculture, roofing, delivery, and warehouse workers; OSHA-recognized occupational exposure.
People with substance-use disorders or serious mental illness Frequently overlap with unsheltered homelessness; can blunt physiological heat response.
Infants and young children Vehicular-heatstroke deaths; AZ has had multiple per year.
People dependent on electric medical equipment Power outages during heat waves are double jeopardy.
Renters in older multifamily housing without functioning AC Maricopa data flags AC-out / no-AC indoor deaths.
Tribal community members, especially rural Compounded by housing-quality gaps and limited cooling-center proximity.
Migrants and asylum-seekers in border regions Documented heat-mortality clusters in Pima, Yuma, and Cochise counties.

Risk factors that compound

  • Social isolation (living alone).
  • Chronic disease (cardiovascular, diabetes, renal).
  • Medications that impair thermoregulation (antipsychotics, anticholinergics, diuretics, stimulants).
  • Urban-heat-island intensity at the census-block level — strongly correlated with historical disinvestment.
  • Energy insecurity (utility shut-off history; can’t afford to run AC).
  • Limited mobility / no transportation to reach a cooling center.

Implications for the participatory system

  • Reach the unsheltered population through CHWs and outreach teams, not just web maps. Phone availability is a major filter.
  • Indoor deaths matter as much as outdoor ones. Surveillance and outreach must include people in housing without working AC.
  • Equity-weighted alerts. NWS HeatRisk + UA / MAG vulnerability indices should be combined so that warnings prioritize the highest- burden ZIP codes.
  • Tribal co-design is non-negotiable. Inter Tribal Council of Arizona TEC and the CLIMAS tribal-resilience program are the natural partners.
  • Occupational integration. ADOSH / ICA, ADA (Arizona Department of Agriculture), and trade associations are partner channels.
