Q1 — How does Arizona track wildlife and vector density?

How does Arizona track wildlife? How do they monitor the density of mosquitoes, ticks and fleas? Rodents?

Starting context

Wildlife and vector tracking in Arizona is split across several agencies that each own a slice of the data:

Wildlife — Arizona Game and Fish Department (AZGFD)

  • Wildlife Health Program investigates morbidity and mortality events in game and non-game species.
  • Annual hunter-harvest surveys provide population indices for big game (deer, elk, pronghorn, javelina, bighorn sheep, turkey).
  • AZGFD operates aerial surveys (helicopter) and ground transects for big-game density.
  • Wildlife mortality reports can be submitted by the public via the AZGFD reporting channels.

Mosquito density — county vector-control programs (with ADHS aggregation)

  • Mosquito surveillance in Arizona is largely county-operated: Maricopa County Environmental Services Department (Vector Control), Pima County (Pima County Health Department vector control), Yuma, Pinal, and Coconino each run their own trapping.
  • Typical trap network uses CDC light traps with CO₂ (dry ice) and gravid traps for Culex.
  • Pools are tested for West Nile virus (WNV) and St. Louis encephalitis (SLE); some counties test for Aedes aegypti expansion.
  • Counties report weekly to ADHS, which publishes an Arbovirus Surveillance Summary during transmission season.

Ticks and fleas — mixed

  • No statewide systematic tick or flea-density program comparable to the mosquito network.
  • Plague (Yersinia pestis) is endemic in northern Arizona; ADHS and county health track flea/rodent die-offs (especially prairie-dog and ground-squirrel colonies) reactively.
  • Tick-borne Rocky Mountain spotted fever has focal outbreaks on tribal lands (the brown dog tick, Rhipicephalus sanguineus, is the vector). Surveillance is largely passive / case-driven.

Rodents

  • ADHS hantavirus surveillance is case-driven (Sin Nombre virus).
  • AZGFD tracks population die-offs in prairie dogs as a plague indicator.
  • NEON (see resources) runs standardized small-mammal box-trapping at its Arizona terrestrial sites and tests a subset of captured rodents for selected pathogens.

Data gaps the team could target

  • No unified statewide vector-density dataset across all counties.
  • Tick surveillance is essentially absent outside reactive outbreak investigations.
  • Wildlife mortality reports are siloed across AZGFD, county health, and rehabbers — and rarely cross-referenced with vector data.
  • Tribal lands are under-represented in publicly available data.
